TOKEN-LEVEL ANNOTATION

1. Parts of Speech

What role does each word play?

A token is a unit of text, often a word or punctuation mark. A part-of-speech (POS) tag describes its grammatical role in this context. Click a token to inspect it. A capital letter or word ending is a clue, not proof of a tag.

Definition. A part-of-speech tag belongs to a token in a sentence, not permanently to a word in a dictionary. In "the record," record is a noun. In "they record," it is a verb.
Why use it? POS labels let you ask a more focused question than "which words are common?" For example, collect noun-based topics, look for adjective-noun descriptions, or identify verbs before querying grammatical relationships.

Token

approved is the exact text that occurred. Its position helps you return to the evidence.

POS and fine tag

VERB is a broad category. VBD, in a Penn-style tagset, additionally marks a past-tense verb.

Lemma

approve groups a form with its base word. Lowercasing Approved produces approved, not its lemma.

POS distribution

Counts include all annotated tokens, including punctuation.

View the token table and tag explanations
Coarse labels such as NOUN and VERB are used throughout this workbench. NLTK's default tags are finer-grained Penn Treebank labels such as NN, NNS, and VBD. Its optional “universal” tagset is not identical to spaCy's UPOS labels.

FROM TOKENS TO PHRASES

2. Chunking & Phrase Patterns

Which nearby words belong together?

Chunking groups neighboring tagged tokens into shallow phrases. A noun phrase can include a determiner, adjectives, and more than one noun. These rules read POS labels, not the meaning of a sentence.

Definition. A chunk is a shallow group of neighboring tokens. A POS-pattern rule selects a sequence of labels; it is not a full sentence parse and does not know whether a phrase is meaningful in your domain.
Why use it? Phrase extraction keeps words together when a single word would lose the topic: customer service, language model, or new analytics program.
Work through one phrase: The/DET new/ADJ analytics/NOUN program/NOUN

ADJ NOUN returns new analytics. ADJ NOUN+ returns new analytics program. DET? ADJ* NOUN+ can include The as well.

? = optional, * = zero or more, + = one or more. Separate tags with spaces. Patterns cannot cross sentence boundaries. This is a small POS-pattern language, not full regular expressions.

Read the pattern one piece at a time

This trace explains the first current match. The matcher takes the longest permitted run, then backs up if a later required item cannot match. A rule cannot cross a sentence boundary.

Why a short rule may miss part of a phrase

In “great customer service,” ADJ NOUN finds “great customer” and stops before “service.” ADJ NOUN+ can include both nouns. A longer match is not automatically more accurate: compare its boundaries with what you intend to extract.

spaCy's doc.noun_chunks uses dependency information. It is not the same operation as matching a sequence of POS tags.

Pattern reference and common boundary mistakes
PatternRead it asUseful test
ADJ NOUNExactly one adjective followed by one common noun.A simple descriptive pair; it may truncate a longer phrase.
DET? ADJ* NOUN+Optional determiner, zero or more adjectives, one or more nouns.Try the new analytics program and students.

Choose the annotation layer intentionally. NLTK RegexpParser groups supplied POS-tagged tokens using a grammar. spaCy Matcher searches token attributes. spaCy doc.noun_chunks obtains base noun phrases from a dependency parse. These are related tools, not interchangeable operations.

GRAMMATICAL RELATIONSHIPS

3. Dependency Relationships

Who did what to whom?

A dependency connects a head word to a dependent word and labels their relationship. The main predicate is marked ROOT. Read each arrow here as head → dependent. Select a token or an arc to inspect the relationship.

Definition. POS labels say what kind of word a token is. Dependencies say how tokens connect. In "Students analyze reviews," Students and reviews are both nouns, but only one is the subject.
Why use it? A grammatical relationship can connect words that are not adjacent. You can query the object of analyze even when an adverb appears between the verb and its object.

Find the root

Find the main predicate in each sentence. Its stored head points to itself, but the diagram leaves out that self-loop.

Read the arrow

Here an arrow goes head → dependent. The relation describes the dependent's connection to that head.

Check the whole clause

Look for auxiliaries, passive constructions, and negation before describing the actor or claiming that an event occurred.

Follow a token back to the root

This path navigates from dependent to head, opposite the arrow direction in the diagram. It reads the stored parse; it does not infer a new one.

Active voice, passive voice, and negation

“The manager approved the request” and “The request was approved by the manager” describe a similar action but have different grammatical subjects. A subject is not always the actor. “The manager did not approve the request” adds negation and must not be stored as an approval.

Label schemes differ. These examples use spaCy English-style labels such as dobj and nsubjpass. Other schemes often use obj and nsubj:pass. The relationships matter more than memorizing one spelling.

Same event description, different grammatical subject

The manager approved the request. Subject: manager. Object: request.

The request was approved by the manager. Subject: request. The manager appears in a by-agent phrase. There is no direct object in this simple passive sentence.

Attachment can be ambiguous. In "The analyst saw the researcher with a telescope," the telescope could be the viewing instrument or something the researcher had. The supplied example chooses one interpretation. A parse is a proposed structure, not evidence that the interpretation is true.

Do not turn a simple query into a universal extractor. Coordination, pronouns, embedded clauses, and sentences such as "The manager did not promise to approve the request" require more than checking for any occurrence of not.

MEANINGFUL TEXT SPANS

4. Named Entity Recognition

Which spans identify people, places, or things?

Named entity recognition (NER) finds a span of text and assigns a type, such as PERSON, ORG, or GPE. A gazetteer is a dictionary of known names. Compare reference/model annotations with a transparent dictionary-and-date baseline.

Definition. Entity recognition predicts both a span and a type. A PERSON may span two tokens, while a DATE may contain numbers, punctuation, or ordinary words.
Why use it? An event announcement can become a set of candidate people, organizations, locations, and dates. Review these candidates before linking them into an event record.
Two questions, not one:

For Northstar Labs, ask "Where does the name start and end?" and "Which type fits?" Labeling only Northstar as ORG still misses part of the intended span. A rule that finds a name does not verify the organization or link it to a database identifier.

Edit the gazetteer and date rules

Matching settings

Supported dates: YYYY-MM-DD, MM/DD/YYYY, month + day, and weekday names. MM/DD/YYYY is interpreted as month/day/year. Dates without a year are not resolved. All occurrences are matched; longer overlapping spans take priority within a layer.

Understand span boundaries and IOB labels

A span has a start and an end. Offsets here count Unicode characters from zero, and the end is excluded. The text at text[start:end] should equal the entity. In IOB notation, B begins an entity, I continues it, and O is outside it.

A matching label is not enough when the boundaries are wrong. “Northstar” and “Northstar Labs” are different spans. A dictionary can miss an unfamiliar name, while a trained model can predict an incorrect name or type. Neither output is automatically a verified fact.

Try a span-masking preview

Replace selected entity spans with placeholders. This uses the current entity layer and type filter, not an additional detector. It demonstrates what a span-based transformation does.

Not guaranteed anonymization. Missed entities and identifying context can remain. Repeated mentions receive separate placeholders; the preview does not resolve whether two mentions refer to the same person.
Dictionary, model, boundaries, and IOB: what to check
Layer or methodWhat it contributesWhat can go wrong
Dictionary / gazetteerMatches known strings you explicitly list.New names are missed; an ambiguous name may match in the wrong context.
Date patternFinds a predictable text shape.A matched format can contain an impossible date. A weekday alone needs document context to resolve.
Trained NER componentPredicts spans and types from context.A familiar type may still be missed or assigned to the wrong span.
Entity linkingMaps a mention to a specific record or identity.This workbench does not perform entity linking or identity verification.

Keep the offset convention. The interval [start, end) includes the start position and excludes the end. These workbench exports count Unicode code points, matching Python string slicing. A browser's raw JavaScript string indices count UTF-16 code units, which can differ for characters such as emoji. The interface converts offsets before exporting.

IOB is about token boundaries. B-PERSON begins a person span, I-PERSON continues it, and O is outside any span. A character match that splits a token cannot be cleanly represented with one IOB label per token. Inspect the boundary warning rather than silently trimming it.

FROM ANNOTATIONS TO DATA

5. Linguistic Features

What can you summarize or extract?

Annotations can become columns in a dataset: noun counts, verb rates, entity counts, or grammatical patterns. Keep the original text and the analysis method so every number can be checked.

Definition. A linguistic feature is a measurement derived from annotations. Counts depend on document length; rates use an explicit denominator so the measurement can be interpreted.
Why use it? Features can help summarize collections or serve as inputs to a later analysis. Preserve the annotation source so you can tell whether a change came from the text, the rules, or a different model.
Interpretation matters. A high adjective rate does not establish sentiment, and an entity count does not show whether an announcement is correct. These are descriptive features, not conclusions.
From a measurement to a responsible interpretation
MeasurementA useful interpretationAn unsupported conclusion
More NOUN / PROPN tokensThis annotation has more noun-like tokens.The document is more accurate or more important.
Higher adjective rateA larger fraction of word-like tokens is tagged ADJ.The sentiment is positive.
More PERSON spansMore person-name mentions were detected.More distinct people are involved.
No dependency parseDependency-derived features are unavailable.There are zero negation or passive constructions.
Higher unknown-POS rateThe baseline leaves more tokens unresolved.The author used incorrect language.

Do not mix denominators. Here, noun rate includes NOUN and PROPN. Verb rate includes VERB but not AUX. Word-like tokens exclude PUNCT and SYM but retain X. A rate is computed from those choices, not from an unstated count of "words."

Collection-level rates. Summing all noun tokens and dividing by all word-like tokens gives a length-weighted rate. Averaging per-document noun rates weights every document equally. Both are defensible summaries, but they answer different questions.

6. Error Analysis: What Counts as Correct?

A plausible-looking annotation is not enough. Compare predictions with a small, supplied reference. Here you can correct POS tags and entity boundaries, then see exactly which scores change.

Reference versus prediction. Compare the selected sentence with its provided reference annotations. Start with browser predictions, your current matching analysis, or a practice answer with errors to correct. The scores describe this sentence, not the overall accuracy of spaCy or NLTK.

POS: compare aligned tokens

Each row uses a matching character interval. Change a predicted label to correct it. An unaligned token is reported as a mismatch rather than treated as a correct tag.

See the POS confusion counts

Rows are reference labels. Columns are predicted labels. A diagonal entry is a correct tag; an off-diagonal entry is a disagreement.

Entities: the full span and label must match

Use one prediction per line: start|end|TYPE. End positions are exclusive. Empty input means no predicted entities. The score uses exact (start, end, type) matches, not just the visible name.

Reference spans

How to read the numbers

Precision

TP / (TP + FP)
Of the entities predicted, how many exactly match the reference?

Recall

TP / (TP + FN)
Of the reference entities, how many were found?

F1

2TP / (2TP + FP + FN)
Balances exact-match precision and recall.

A wrong boundary can create both a false positive and a false negative: the predicted span is not in the reference, and the reference span was not found. We show "Not defined" for a zero denominator rather than inventing a perfect score.

Worked arithmetic. Suppose a reference contains three entities and a system predicts four, with two exact matches. TP = 2, FP = 2, FN = 1; precision = 50%, recall = 66.7%, and F1 = 57.1%.

This exercise uses provided reference annotations, not a held-out test set. See NLTK's information-extraction discussion for the distinction between token and span evaluation.

THE MODULE'S APPLIED CASE

Candidate fields from event announcements

The module uses event announcements to practice extraction. Here, each row gathers possible people, organizations, places, and dates from a document. These are candidates, not verified event records. When a document mentions two events, this table does not know which date belongs to which event.

Entity totals in this dataset view use each document's analysis annotations, not the text explorer's display filter or custom gazetteer overlay.

Additional dataset checks

Compare groups and find documents worth reviewing

Group summaries use the documents actually analyzed, after your row limit and deduplication choice. They do not silently describe the entire uploaded file.

Pooled noun rate = all NOUN + PROPN tokens in the group / all word-like tokens in the group. Entity mentions are not unique people or organizations. Rates from different annotation methods should not be treated as directly comparable model performance.

Before exporting a feature table

Review missingness. A blank negation feature means the document has no dependency parse. It does not mean the sentence is affirmative.

Review duplicates. A repeated announcement may be a legitimate repeat, a copy, or a data error. Choose what counts as one document before interpreting totals.

Review the language and domain. The supplied rules and model examples are English-focused. Domain terms, abbreviations, and unfamiliar names can produce unresolved or incorrect labels.

Retain evidence. The export keeps the input text, document identifier, row position, and source of annotations. Validate downstream conclusions against those fields.

Keep the module's event-announcement case small enough to inspect by hand. The target is a table of candidate fields, not an automatically verified calendar.

Example announcement

Maya Chen will host a workshop at Northstar Labs in Harbor City on October 4.

The names and places in this example are fictional. Notice that October 4 has no year.

1

Define the fields before writing a rule

Keep the original text, person mentions, organization mentions, place mentions, date text, and the action phrase. Record the annotation method as well.

2

Inspect tokens and POS labels

Find host as the main verb and will as its auxiliary in this reference. "Will host" describes a planned event; the text does not report that it already happened.

3

Extract candidate spans

PERSON: Maya Chen. ORG: Northstar Labs. GPE: Harbor City. DATE: October 4. Keep each span's original text and start/end offsets.

4

Read relationships before assigning roles

An organization mentioned after "at" is not automatically the organizer. Entity recognition alone does not tell you which organization hosts the workshop.

5

There is no explicit year, time, registration link, or verified venue address. Leave those fields unresolved rather than inventing values.

Scale to the sample CSV, then inspect failures
